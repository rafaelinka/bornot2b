An Airport Buys More Than Clean Floors

Luotea and Finavia: trained people, site access, and the authority to change priorities are all part of service capacity.

A cleaning contract invites a simple picture: floor area multiplied by a rate per square foot. In an airport, that can miss the real purchase. The right person needs to be in the right place during the available working window. The customer buys completed work, but also the supplier’s ability to restore the desired condition when demand changes.

What the agreement establishes

On November 23, Luotea announced that it had won Finavia’s tender to clean the Schengen area of Helsinki Airport. Work starts March 1, 2027; the agreement continues until terminated. It provides for around-the-clock shifts and security-cleared backup staff. A Finavia representative confirmed the expanded relationship. Rates, team size, and penalties are undisclosed; there are no public results from the new service yet.

Backup capacity is part of the service

Our interpretation is that service capacity consists of more than the people on the normal schedule. It also requires the ability to call a suitable replacement, get that person onto the site, and communicate the job. Someone who is free on a calendar and someone who is ready to work at this particular facility are different resources. The latter needs preparation, access, and availability to come together.

This changes the pricing question. A buyer may count only the hours when work is visible. The supplier has to maintain the ability to perform it when the plan breaks down. If backup capacity has no understood economic place, it becomes either a hidden burden on the contractor or a resource that is missing when needed. We do not know how this contract pays for it.

A roster alone does not establish responsiveness. Notification, travel, instruction, and coordination separate an available employee from a resolved problem.

The same area can be a different job

Consider a hypothetical disruption. Delayed flights change passenger flows, and planned cleaning collides with unexpected demand. The building has not grown. But someone must now choose between waiting, reassigning staff, and temporarily limiting access. This is an illustration, not an account of an incident in Helsinki.

An area-cleaned measure distinguishes these situations poorly. It can help plan routine work, but it does not explain how long a particular location remained unusable. An average completion time can also conceal a critical exception. A useful measure connects the work to its consequence: what was unavailable, for how long, and who could change the priority?

We lack the evidence to propose thresholds for this airport. The distinction is between activity completed and the condition the buyer wanted. Confuse them, and a contractor can meet its plan while the customer remains dissatisfied. Authority matters too: a fully staffed team can respond slowly if nobody can reassign it.

A robot does not allocate responsibility

Technology in a service like this deserves evaluation at the level of a specific task. A machine may change how repetitive work is performed. That does not establish that it independently decides where the remaining team should go, how an exception should be closed, or who accepts the result. The process has to explain those handoffs regardless of the equipment used.

It is especially easy to confuse deployment with impact. Introducing technology is an observable event. Lower costs or better quality require comparable measurements and attention to everything that changed alongside it: schedules, area, user traffic, and staffing. The announcement does not supply results from which we can calculate that return. We have not independently tested the equipment.

A counterexample: the buyer brings cleaning back in-house

Context outside the news week. A Helsinki city decision dated November 12, 2026, published on November 13, ends Luotea Siivous Oy’s eastern-district contract for social, health and rescue facilities on May 13, 2027. The city will bring the cleaning in-house. The document specifies no minimum purchase or exclusivity and six months’ notice without a stated reason. This is a city contract, not Finavia’s; the decision does not attribute termination to poor service.

For a contractor, that raises a distinct commercial question: how does the duration of its own costs compare with the period of committed purchasing? If a team and equipment are needed for years but demand can fall earlier, multiplying one month’s revenue by a framework’s full term will not produce a sound forecast. Model the reduction in work: which staff and machines can move, which costs remain, and how long the transition takes. This is our interpretation, not a calculation of Luotea’s losses.

The buyer has a transition to manage too. Bringing work in-house can require recruitment, equipment and a shift leader before the external agreement ends. Exit provisions therefore form part of service design. Neither the six-month notice nor the city’s other terms can be transferred to Finavia; they are not disclosed in the public announcement of the airport agreement.

More backup is not always better

Assess backup capacity alongside the ability to redeploy it. A prepared employee who can work at several suitable sites and one cleared for only one site create different ongoing costs. Pooling teams helps only when access permissions, shifts and travel distances fit; the same spare shift cannot be promised to two customers simultaneously.

The commercial relationship faces two tests: can the supplier perform on a difficult day, and what happens to each party’s costs if demand changes or the agreement ends? Finavia’s announcement raises the first; a separate city buyer’s document raises the second. Performance under the new airport agreement can be assessed after work begins.
