What has to happen before the last truck leaves

Warehouse & Distribution Science. Eight ideas from the full text on picking, loading and the gaps that can make a late order miss its truck.

John J. Bartholdi III and Steven T. Hackman · Georgia Tech · 2019 · 7 min read

A backup needs checking before a switch. A conceptual illustration for the book discussion.

Warehouse & Distribution Science, John J. Bartholdi III and Steven T. Hackman, Georgia Tech, release 0.98.1, 2019. The book treats a warehouse as a series of handoffs. Inventory becomes an order line, items become a parcel, and parcels become a load. Each stage has its own capacity and wait. The buyer of a logistics service needs a deadline the entire chain can meet. Here are eight ideas from the book and our examples of how to use them.

1. Measure waiting where it happens

§ 2.5, pp. 18–19

The authors apply Little’s Law to warehouse flows. In steady state, the average number of units within a defined process equals the average flow rate multiplied by the average time spent in the process. The same relationship can describe an entire facility or a single stage if the boundaries and units are consistent.

In practice. If an average of 60 cartons are between packing and loading and the stage consistently handles 120 an hour, the average time there is 30 minutes. These are illustrative numbers. To find out how many parcels missed the last truck, look at individual timestamps and their handoff deadlines, especially near the end of the shift.

2. Follow the order through dispatch

§§ 3.3–3.5, pp. 25–29

Picking several orders in one trip increases the number of picks per metre walked. The items then have to be assigned to their individual orders, either on the cart or at a separate sorting stage. Packing gives staff a chance to verify completeness. A parcel-ready scan and a truck-departure scan record different events.

In practice. Compare two picking methods using elapsed time from release to readiness for dispatch, error rate and parcels per order. If the cart comes back sooner under batch picking but the parcels wait longer at sorting, the full cycle will show it.

3. Price the replenishment of fast-pick slots

Chapters 7–8, pp. 77–88 and 99–123

Storing a product near the picker saves travel on each visit. A small slot can empty sooner and require more trips from reserve stock. The authors consider pick frequency, product flow and allocated space together. Their continuous model gives a starting point that has to fit real cartons, shelves and lanes.

In practice. Start with two SKUs that get the same number of picks. Suppose one moves ten cases a week and the other moves two. With equal slots they impose different replenishment workloads. Before moving both into the fast-pick area, compare the minutes saved on picking with the minutes spent replenishing and the delays when a slot runs empty.

4. Test picking routes on actual order profiles

Chapters 10–11, pp. 169–170 and 181–186

The value of route optimisation depends on the order mix. A picker can often find a short path for one to three items. An order that covers most of the building also leaves little room to shorten the route. The authors show a sequential picking model in which a buffer ahead of a busy station can increase work in progress and reduce total output.

In practice. Before buying a routing module, give the vendor three samples: small orders, medium-sized orders with scattered items, and large orders. Compare how easy the instructions are to follow, when the full order is ready and what happens to the queue at the next stage.

5. Check whether the dock can keep up with picking

Chapters 12–13 and § 16.3, pp. 200–204, 221–223 and 269–270

At a carousel, the picker sometimes waits for equipment and the equipment sometimes waits for the picker. A similar dependency appears between the flow of packed parcels and trailer loading. The authors explicitly link order cut-offs to a rush of orders just before the deadline. As arrivals approach loading capacity, delays increase. Freight staged at the dock can slow loading further.

In practice. For an evening window like the one in the ASOS story, inspect the half-hour before the last truck. In a hypothetical example, 150 parcels become ready in 30 minutes and loading can take 120. If the queue starts empty and there is no other route out, at least 30 remain at the end. Faster picking adds to that remainder unless the warehouse and carrier change capacity or timing.

6. Agree on the units before comparing warehouses

Chapters 14–15, pp. 233–240 and 255–257

In the authors’ drugstore example, a product can rank differently by pieces, cartons and picks. Pick lines per hour also depend on whether the team handles cases or individual units. The opening of chapter 14 sets the starting point: Understand the customer orders, which drive the system.

In practice. Give vendors an anonymised order profile with the selling unit, pack size and split between piece, case and pallet picks. Compare quotes on the same order mix. Review speed alongside accuracy, cost and the share of complete orders handed over by the deadline.

7. When consolidating facilities, count the full journey

§ 15.3, § 16.1 and chapter 17, pp. 264–265, 267–268 and 277–284

Pooling stock can reduce the need to duplicate safety inventory. A larger building can add travel if the process stays the same. The authors examine this using a comparison of 36 warehouses from one company with similar products and resources. The result describes that network. The chapter on regional differences shows how land, labour and urban transport shape facility decisions.

In practice. When comparing one central warehouse with two regional sites, list safety stock, time inside the warehouse, transit time and the last departure for each delivery area. Set the inventory savings beside the number of customers who can actually receive their orders by the required date in each design.

8. Evaluate minimum order quantities alongside inventory

Appendix A, pp. 293–298

The basic economic order quantity model balances the cost of placing orders against the cost of holding stock. The reorder point answers a separate question about when to place an order. In the model it equals expected demand during lead time plus safety stock. Variation in demand and replenishment times matters for that decision.

In practice. If a supplier offers a discount on a larger case, recalculate order frequency, average inventory, capital tied up and obsolescence risk. Then check what happens if replenishment is late. The discount is explicit. The buffer depends on observed lead times and demand variation.

Take this into a joint review

Editorial application to this issue

Take late orders from a few comparable days and reconstruct five timestamps for each one: accepted at checkout, released to the warehouse, picked and packed in full, accepted by the carrier, delivered. Record the planned end of loading and the date promised to the customer separately. The buyer can see which parts of the promise it controls, and the provider can show where more capacity or an earlier signal from the retailer would help. End the meeting with one verifiable cause of a missed deadline and one shared way to measure it the following week.
