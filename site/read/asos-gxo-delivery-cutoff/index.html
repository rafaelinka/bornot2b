The Big Read

The 10:54 p.m. order and the promise of next-day delivery

ASOS and GXO have extended their logistics partnership through 2034. Follow one late-evening order to see what a retailer buys in a long-term contract and how it can tell whether the service is working.

ASOS × GXO · United Kingdom / United States · November 11 · 4 min read

Hardware, documentation and production know-how involve different work. An editorial metaphor.

What the companies disclosed

On 11 November, US logistics provider GXO announced that its partnership with British online retailer ASOS would run through 2034. The agreement covers warehouse fulfilment and transport. The relationship began at a single site in Barnsley in 2013. In GXO’s release, ASOS logistics director Jonny O’Hara links the renewal to the need for flexible, reliable operations. The companies did not disclose the contract value, volume commitments or service targets.

GXO points to one outcome from the previous five years. The operator says changes in how it worked allowed ASOS to extend its order cut-off to 11 p.m. The retailer’s own delivery help page adds a detail that matters. Available options and deadlines are displayed at checkout, depend on the address and time of purchase, and can change during busy periods. A late cut-off describes what the network can offer under certain conditions. The companies did not publish how many orders qualified for the late window and arrived on time.

Where the delivery promise can break

Say a shopper places an order at 10:54 p.m. and sees a next-day delivery date. The warehouse has to release the order for picking, pack it and hand it to the carrier before the vehicle leaves. The 11 p.m. cut-off applies to orders. The companies have not disclosed the vehicle departure time. If the parcel is ready on schedule but misses the vehicle, the warehouse can record a successful pick while the customer still gets a late delivery.

A procurement team can measure this in two ways. Start with the share of eligible baskets shown the next-day option in a given area and time window. Then measure the share of placed orders carrying that promise that were delivered on time. The two belong together. Withdrawing an option at peak times can improve the reported on-time rate while reducing service availability. To investigate a missed delivery, compare events both sides can verify: the promised date, order release, parcel ready time, carrier acceptance and proof of delivery.

What a later cut-off costs

Imagine the retailer takes 300 orders in a newly opened evening window. Of those, 240 would have been placed earlier or the next day anyway. If the other 60 are genuinely additional and each contributes £8 after expected returns and ordinary variable costs, the retailer gains £480. An extra shift and vehicle run cost £400, leaving £80 before other effects. On those assumptions, the window breaks even at 50 incremental orders. The numbers are illustrative. A real trial could compare similar delivery areas and days, while accounting for promotions and seasonality.

ASOS’s own reporting shows why the calculation needs to cover the whole chain. In the year to 30 August 2026, the retailer reported distribution costs of 10.2% of revenue, down from 10.6%, mainly from renegotiated carrier contracts and network retenders. Warehouse costs fell from 10.4% to 9.5% of revenue, with the Atlanta closure and Berlin automation among the factors. Fulfilling US orders from Barnsley after Atlanta closed raised distribution costs, even as fulfilment through the UK network lowered warehouse cost per unit. These are ASOS network figures. GXO’s contribution and the economics of the November renewal were not separately disclosed.

What to settle at renewal

The retailer can bring promise availability, on-time delivery and cost per order after returns into the same review. The contract can spell out who pays for peak capacity, who owns the equipment and data, and how operations would transfer if the relationship ended. For the supplier, the strongest case for renewal is evidence of what the full service delivers under comparable conditions, alongside the capacity and operating decisions that keep it working through peak demand.
