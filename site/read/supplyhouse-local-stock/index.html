Routes to Market

Which parts earn a place in the next-day network?

SupplyHouse plans a fifth US fulfillment center. For tradespeople, its value depends on which parts arrive in time for a job.

SupplyHouse · United States · November 12 · 2 min read

Hardware, documentation and production know-how involve different work. An editorial metaphor.

What was announced

On November 12, plumbing, HVAC and electrical distributor SupplyHouse announced a 715,000-square-foot center near Locust Grove, Georgia, with opening planned for 2027. The company expects the site to extend one-day delivery coverage in Georgia, Florida and the Carolinas. Georgia’s governor’s office estimates an investment of US$19 million and 330 jobs. SupplyHouse says it offers about 350,000 products across its business. It has yet to disclose which of those products the new site will stock or the delivery promise by ZIP code.

B(ornot)2B analysis

Count the parts that travel together

A contractor may need a valve, its fittings and the correct connector for the same visit. If two items come from the local warehouse and the third comes from another state, the contractor can still lose the slot. In a hypothetical tender, a buyer could send 30 anonymized baskets and ask which can arrive complete by tomorrow, the ordering deadline and the fallback if one line is unavailable. That yields a testable service promise across typical and urgent jobs.

Price the inventory close to the job

For a distributor, putting a line in Georgia saves transit time on eligible orders but can add stock and replenishment costs. A practical starting point is to rank parts by local emergency demand and by how often they appear in the same basket. After opening, track complete next-day baskets, split shipments and emergency purchases by route. Warehouse size and catalog size describe capacity and assortment. The initial test is whether local stock can cover the jobs contractors actually book.
