Green Volt: A Factory Slot Needs a Shared Project Window

A manufacturer, port, and buyer can each meet their own schedule and still miss one another. Reservation value depends on compatible windows and authority to release the next expenditure.

Each participant can have a sound contract and a feasible schedule of its own. A complex project succeeds when those schedules meet. Capacity reservation purchases something valuable, but its value depends on decisions outside the factory.

What the participants disclosed

On November 24, 2026, Green Volt and Port of Aberdeen announced two arrangements. An agreement with Siemens Gamesa reserves manufacturing capacity and engineering resources for 28 turbines rated at more than 15 MW each. A separate letter of intent concerns port handling of anchors and mooring equipment; the expected investment and expenditure package is £15 million. Green Volt describes the reservation as a multimillion-pound agreement and says the port investment remains subject to final confirmation. Deposits, payment schedules, cancellation provisions, and delay liability are undisclosed. The developer and port carry a joint announcement, not independent verification of delivery.

The following is our analysis. It does not describe unpublished terms of either arrangement.

The buyer needs a shared working window

This story contains two distinct subjects: manufacturing capacity and engineering resources at the supplier, and a port letter concerning anchors and mooring equipment. They are not one contract for a completed project. The figures of 28 turbines and £15 million do not reveal when the participants can perform connected operations.

Our focus here is schedule compatibility. The Amylyx cover story examines prepayments and binding forecasts. Green Volt raises the next question: what turns an individual reservation into coordinated work by several participants? The announcements do not let us reconstruct the project calendar or authority to release the next stage.

Two feasible schedules may still miss each other

Consider a hypothetical industrial project, not Green Volt. A manufacturer can deliver a module on June 1; the receiving site is ready on August 1. Finishing production two weeks earlier will not advance the next operation: the module stays in storage longer. If the site is ready on May 1, the same manufacturing improvement may change the project result.

In a basic model, the earliest start is the latest readiness date among required resources. This is not a complete project schedule: subsequent work, repeat operations, and resource limits remain. It simply prevents a team from adding every contractor's “weeks saved” into a promise to accelerate the project.

The model has another limit. It assumes a ready resource remains available while the others catch up. A reserved crew or site may move to another customer first. In that case, the last readiness date is no longer enough.

A readiness date is not an availability window

A separate hypothetical example, not Green Volt’s calendar, not Green Volt's calendar. One uninterrupted operation needs cargo, a site, and a crew. It takes eight days. All boundaries below are the start of the stated day; the ending date is excluded.

ResourceAvailable fromAvailable until
Cargo for the operationJune 1July 1
Receiving siteJune 12June 26
Crew with required equipmentJune 15June 21

All three first meet on June 15, but the crew leaves at the beginning of June 21. The shared window lasts six days; the operation needs eight. Starting earlier is impossible, and starting on June 15 is insufficient. Three readiness confirmations can therefore coexist with an unworkable plan.

If the crew agrees to remain until the beginning of June 23, eight days become available from June 15 to June 23. Receiving the cargo a week earlier instead leaves the shared window at six days: it was already available before the crew arrived. All else equal, the useful comparison is the cost of two extra crew days against the cost and consequences of the next available period.

In general, the overlap begins at the latest starting boundary and ends at the earliest ending boundary. An end before the start means no overlap. A positive overlap must still accommodate the entire uninterrupted operation. A common starting date does not prove that the work can finish.

We do not know Green Volt's window boundaries. Rescheduling flexibility and window duration are therefore subjects for follow-up, not established advantages of the agreement. A week of postponement flexibility may be worth more than a week of faster manufacturing; widening a receiving site's window may be cheaper. The answer depends on the calendar and the price of a change.

Which part of the reservation can move?

A “flexible reservation” can conceal three different rights: move the date, use capacity for a different configuration, or release it for another customer. One does not imply another. Even permission to request a move does not oblige the supplier to grant it.

ChangeWhat the terms need to establishWhere a cost arises
Move the same workThe protected date range and notice periodExtension, a lost slot, changes to adjacent schedules
Change the configurationWhich engineering work remains usableRework and renewed approval
Release the reservationWho may resell it, and whether that affects the original customer's paymentUnrecoverable preparation and lost priority

This is our negotiating framework, not known Green Volt rights. It helps to distinguish general-purpose capacity, partly reusable preparation, and work useful almost exclusively to one customer. Those differences affect how much value survives a change and who retains prepared documents.

An option is an economic analogy for preserving a later choice, not a legal description of this agreement. As connected work becomes specific to one configuration and supplier, changing course may become more expensive. The price of an early reservation and the price of retained flexibility are different quantities.

Who is allowed to say “start”?

A shared calendar is insufficient when authority is unclear. One participant may treat a signal as a forecast while another sees permission to begin costly preparation. Cooperation continues, but incurred costs and recognized commitments start to diverge.

For a project of this kind, we propose a short decision register:

DecisionWhat needs to be explicit before action
Freeze the configurationWho approves the version, and which changes require fresh approval
Begin irreversible preparationWhich document authorizes expenditure, and who can issue it
Confirm the shared windowWhich confirmations from other participants support the promise
Change the scheduleWho decides across the chain, and when previous dates cease to apply
Stop or replace a work packageWhat happens to completed work, reserved capacity, and documents

The last responsible decision point must be late enough to use new information and early enough for the supplier to perform. Delay is not automatically flexibility: postponing a decision can simply remove the other party's working time.

Price the shared delay without assuming who caused it

Choosing between extension, rescheduling, and acceleration requires incremental costs against the same baseline calendar. Storage, project staff, hired-resource downtime, and postponed downstream work do not always add together: some rates are inside fixed prices and some replace another expense. Contractual compensation also belongs on a separate line. It changes who bears a loss without necessarily reducing the project's loss.

Hypothetical example, not Green Volt data: a reservation costs 300,000 currency units and reduces the probability of an additional one-month delay from 30% to 10%. That month adds 2 million in costs. The expected reduction is 400,000: 20 percentage points multiplied by 2 million. Subtracting the reservation leaves 100,000 before other effects.

The key assumption is that the reservation preserves a workable shared window, rather than simply accelerating manufacturing. If both production dates leave the project waiting for another site, the 400,000 benefit may disappear. If keeping the reservation also creates 150,000 of expected storage or rescheduling cost, the initial result turns negative.

All amounts and probabilities are invented to explain the method. Contractor estimates cannot simply be multiplied: a common cause may delay several participants. Nor do two extra days in separate agreements automatically create two extra days in the shared window. The consolidated plan needs to show each choice's effect on the operation the project actually needs to complete.

The port has its own economics of waiting

A project buyer wants a site ready for its arrival. A port operator also cares about utilization, competing cargoes, investment recovery, and the ability to offer space to somebody else. The same date can have different values to the two businesses.

Infrastructure useful to many customers retains some value if the first project moves. Highly specific modifications create greater dependence. The headline investment amount cannot distinguish them; the scope and alternative uses matter.

An initial project might also help establish infrastructure that later projects make sustainable. The early customer would then help create a market for future users while potentially competing with them for attractive windows. This is a testable hypothesis about the relationship, not a claim about the port's intentions.

“Who funds construction?” is therefore only one question. Who pays for readiness before the first cargo? Who may use a released window? Does the original customer retain priority after postponement? Those rights can change the value of the same nominal investment.

Four cases against early reservation

First, standard capacity may be readily available and contractors inexpensive to replace. Reservation can create dependence before scarcity exists. The benefit of future access must be weighed against lost choice.

Second, the configuration itself may be uncertain. Early, highly specific engineering can create more rework than time savings. A compatible reservation or separately authorized stages could be better, if the parties permit them.

Third, an unready downstream constraint can turn early equipment into expensive inventory. Reservation might still protect against an even longer queue after that constraint clears, but that is a different scenario.

Fourth, risks can share a cause. A backup contractor relying on the same overloaded resource does not provide the expected independence. Two agreements can rest on one physical capability.

Which documents will show whether the reservation worked?

Changes in commitments and dates are more informative follow-up than repeated announcements of scale. Our three hypotheses need different evidence:

Rescheduling rights preserve the common window better than an early fixed slotAn agreed move, its revised usable duration, and its costExpensive flexibility still fails to make the required operation possible
A joint release rule reduces reworkThe calendar version that authorized preparation and the outcome of that workExtra approval time consumes the benefit
General-purpose and project-specific preparation need different exit rulesWork actually reused after postponement or cancellationClaimed reuse potential never becomes actual use

Until intentions become confirmed obligations, a common interval is protected, and the next irreversible expenditure is authorized, reservation price cannot establish the project result. That does not prove weak preparation by the parties: the necessary documents may exist without being public.

Evidence and limits

Both announcements are dated November 24, 2026; checked November 27. No operating period or delivery result is reported. The port letter's stated scope must not be expanded into all turbine assembly and installation. Reservation is not delivery. Equipment prices, paid amounts, and compensation provisions remain unknown. The announcement's “world first” claim is not necessary to this analysis. We conducted no interviews or performance verification.
