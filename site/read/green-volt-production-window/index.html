Green Volt: A Factory Slot Needs a Shared Project Window

A manufacturer, port, and buyer can each meet their own schedule and still miss one another. Reservation value depends on compatible windows and authority to release the next expenditure.

Each participant can have a sound contract and a feasible schedule of its own. A complex project succeeds when those schedules meet. Capacity reservation purchases something valuable, but its value depends on decisions outside the factory.

What the participants disclosed

On November 24, 2026, Green Volt and Port of Aberdeen announced two arrangements. An agreement with Siemens Gamesa reserves manufacturing capacity and engineering resources for 28 turbines rated at more than 15 MW each. A separate letter of intent concerns port handling of anchors and mooring equipment; the expected investment and expenditure package is £15 million. Green Volt describes the reservation as a multimillion-pound agreement and says the port investment remains subject to final confirmation. Deposits, payment schedules, cancellation provisions, and delay liability are undisclosed. The developer and port carry a joint announcement, not independent verification of delivery.

The following is our analysis. It does not describe unpublished terms of either arrangement.

Buying a sequence of possibilities

An equipment announcement naturally directs attention to units, output, and manufacturer. A complex project's buyer also needs a right to receive a particular action during a particular period. A contractor may be technically capable and commercially unavailable when the customer needs it. Reservation seeks to reduce that uncertainty.

Its usefulness depends on a sequence. Engineering must be settled before the relevant parts are made. Manufactured output must complete necessary checks. The next operation needs space, equipment, and people. An early agreement with one participant does not remove those dependencies.

The buyer's first question is therefore which subsequent decisions the reservation enables. Can it support an order for connected components? A promise to the next contractor? Irreversible preparatory work? Or does it currently allow only further engineering? The same word, “reservation,” can conceal very different permissions. The Green Volt announcement does not let us reconstruct them.

Two feasible schedules may still miss each other

Consider a hypothetical industrial project, not Green Volt. A manufacturer can deliver a module on June 1; the receiving site becomes ready on August 1. Finishing manufacturing two weeks earlier will not advance joint readiness. It may increase storage time. If the site is ready on May 1, the same production improvement could change the overall result.

For an operation requiring all inputs simultaneously, a basic model is:

Earliest start = the latest readiness date among required inputs.

This is not a complete project schedule. It excludes subsequent duration, repeat operations, and resource constraints. Its practical role is to prevent a team from adding up contractors' “weeks saved” without checking dependencies. Improving a noncritical step may create resilience, but not an earlier start.

In this model, calculate the effect of a reservation twice: first on the individual supplier's obligation, then on the project outcome under its remaining constraints. If the second effect is zero, the reservation might still insure against a future change. That insurance needs a separate argument.

A readiness date is not an availability window

Even a complete set of readiness dates misses another risk: several resources must be available together. One site may hold capacity for a month and another contractor for only a week. Missing the shared interval can send the project back into a queue even if every participant met its own obligation.

Represent each participant's acceptable period as an interval from the start to the end of its resource availability. A common interval exists if the latest early date is no later than the earliest late date. If the operation needs uninterrupted work, that overlap must also accommodate its duration.

That reframes negotiation. A week of postponement flexibility may be more useful than a week of faster production. Widening the receiving site's window may cost less than accelerating the manufacturer. The purchased benefit is schedule compatibility.

We do not know Green Volt's window boundaries, transfer rights, or extension costs. Those details would reveal more about reservation economics than another total of nameplate capacity or announced expenditure.

Reservation can protect choice and narrow it

Early agreement reduces the risk that another customer takes the slot. It may also tie the project more tightly to one configuration and supplier. Each connected investment can make switching more expensive.

Our framework separates three layers: capacity that can readily serve another customer; preparatory work that is partly reusable; and decisions useful almost exclusively to one project. This is an analytical classification, not a known breakdown of the participants' costs.

Each layer raises a different question. How quickly could released capacity be resold? How much engineering transfers? Who receives prepared documents if cooperation ends? At what point does a neutral reservation become a difficult-to-exit commitment?

An “option” is a useful economic analogy for the ability to decide later. Without the agreement, it is not a legal characterization. If shifting a date requires another party's consent or creates large losses, the buyer has less freedom than the analogy suggests.

Who is allowed to say “start”?

A shared calendar is insufficient when authority is unclear. One participant may treat a signal as a forecast while another sees permission to begin costly preparation. Cooperation continues, but incurred costs and recognized commitments start to diverge.

For a project of this kind, we propose a short decision register:

DecisionWhat needs to be explicit before action
Freeze the configurationWho approves the version, and which changes require fresh approval
Begin irreversible preparationWhich document authorizes expenditure, and who can issue it
Confirm the shared windowWhich confirmations from other participants support the promise
Change the scheduleWho decides across the chain, and when previous dates cease to apply
Stop or replace a work packageWhat happens to completed work, reserved capacity, and documents

The last responsible decision point must be late enough to use new information and early enough for the supplier to perform. Delay is not automatically flexibility: postponing a decision can simply remove the other party's working time.

Price the shared delay without assuming who caused it

A day's delay can create project staff costs, storage, hired-resource downtime, and a postponed downstream operation. Adding every quoted daily rate is dangerous. Some expenses replace others, some are already inside fixed prices, and some would occur without the delay.

A valid comparison uses incremental cash effects against the same baseline. Counterparty liabilities and the project's economic losses should be shown separately. Compensation reallocates a loss; it does not necessarily reduce the chain's total loss.

Hypothetical example, not Green Volt data: a reservation costs 300,000 currency units. Across agreed scenarios, it reduces the probability of an additional one-month delay from 30% to 10%. The delay would create 2 million of incremental costs. A 20-percentage-point reduction produces an expected benefit of 400,000, leaving an expected advantage of 100,000 before other effects.

If both production dates leave the project waiting for another site, that benefit may disappear. If the reservation also creates 150,000 of expected storage or rescheduling cost, the first calculation turns negative. All figures are invented to explain the method. Nor can project success probabilities be obtained by multiplying contractor estimates without checking whether their risks are dependent.

The port has its own economics of waiting

A project buyer wants a site ready for its arrival. A port operator also cares about utilization, competing cargoes, investment recovery, and the ability to offer space to somebody else. The same date can have different values to the two businesses.

Infrastructure useful to many customers retains some value if the first project moves. Highly specific modifications create greater dependence. The headline investment amount cannot distinguish them; the scope and alternative uses matter.

An initial project might also help establish infrastructure that later projects make sustainable. The early customer would then help create a market for future users while potentially competing with them for attractive windows. This is a testable hypothesis about the relationship, not a claim about the port's intentions.

“Who funds construction?” is therefore only one question. Who pays for readiness before the first cargo? Who may use a released window? Does the original customer retain priority after postponement? Those rights can change the value of the same nominal investment.

Four cases against early reservation

First, standard capacity may be readily available and contractors inexpensive to replace. Reservation can create dependence before scarcity exists. The benefit of future access must be weighed against lost choice.

Second, the configuration itself may be uncertain. Early, highly specific engineering can create more rework than time savings. A compatible reservation or separately authorized stages could be better, if the parties permit them.

Third, an unready downstream constraint can turn early equipment into expensive inventory. Reservation might still protect against an even longer queue after that constraint clears, but that is a different scenario.

Fourth, risks can share a cause. A backup contractor relying on the same overloaded resource does not provide the expected independence. Two agreements can rest on one physical capability.

Three hypotheses worth following

Transferability: the most valuable reservation may be one that allows an agreed move when an adjacent operation changes. Evidence would be shared windows actually preserved and the costs of shifting them. Expensive flexibility that rarely helps would weaken the hypothesis.

Joint readiness: a common release criterion for the next expenditure may reduce rework more than separate attempts to accelerate every contractor. The comparison needs premature starts, their consequences, and any extra approval time that consumes the benefit.

Reservation composition: general-purpose and project-specific preparation may need different exit rules. The test is actual reuse after cancellation or postponement, not the category printed in an agreement.

The next meaningful milestones are changes from intention to obligation, confirmation of a protected shared interval, and authorization of the next irreversible work. Until those are visible, reservation cost cannot be translated into a result for the whole project.

Evidence and limits

Both announcements are dated November 24, 2026; checked November 27. No operating period or delivery result is reported. The port letter's stated scope must not be expanded into all turbine assembly and installation. Reservation is not delivery. Equipment prices, paid amounts, and compensation provisions remain unknown. The announcement's “world first” claim is not necessary to this analysis. We conducted no interviews or performance verification.
