Routes to Market

The customer said yes. When does the business begin?

Karat Packaging reported winning more business with the same chains before it began shipping selected products to them. What turns a commercial agreement into regular deliveries and payment?

Karat Packaging · United States · November 5 · 5 min read

A buyer reviews the range while a supplier prepares a shipment. A composite scene, not a named chain.

The sequence that matters

In its November 5 release, Karat says it secured four chain accounts in Q2 and won additional business across multiple product lines with those accounts in Q3. Shipments of selected products began in October. Shipments tied to the additional business are expected in early 2027. The release does not name the chains or disclose quantities or purchase commitments.

B(ornot)2B analysis

The factual basis appears at the start. We then examine incentives, risks and possible actions. Examples do not describe these companies’ undisclosed contract provisions.

The additional business preceded the first shipments

The additional business came before the reported start of shipments. The sequence gives us no basis to credit it to successful delivery. The chains might have been consolidating suppliers, completing a broader tender or adding items during onboarding. Karat did not specify the items or say whether the agreements expanded the range or increased volume.

For a supplier reading this story, the question is what the customer has actually agreed to do next. A relationship may be commercially promising while its operational obligations remain incomplete. Adding a product to a discussion, approving its specification and placing a binding order give a supplier very different grounds for committing inventory.

One account contains several decisions

Treat the following as a model for examining a chain account, rather than Karat’s disclosed process. A category team may approve the commercial range. Operations must establish where goods are received and which sites use them. Technical staff may need to confirm compatibility. Finance must establish how an invoice becomes payable. One enthusiastic contact cannot necessarily resolve every dependency.

The salesperson’s task is to connect these decisions. A cup can fit the specification while its lid, case quantity or delivery format still needs work. These are examples of questions to settle, not claims about defects in this case. Each unresolved detail can delay a purchase that both sides still want.

The buyer can benefit from managing fewer commercial relationships. The supplier takes on a broader promise. That can be attractive when products share stock, deliveries and support. It can become costly when every added line requires a different operating setup. The size of the range alone cannot settle the trade-off.

How to plan the first delivery wave and the next

A wider assortment changes the shape of the launch. Some products may be ready while others require production, packaging or transport. Launching everything together can simplify communication but make the whole start depend on the slowest item. Separate waves can bring revenue sooner, at the cost of more coordination and potentially smaller, more expensive deliveries.

The choice needs an agreed boundary. Which products and receiving locations belong in the first wave? Can the buyer place orders for those items independently? What must be ready before the next wave opens? A shared launch record makes this sequence visible. Counting approved products without those dates gives a poor picture of the work still required.

The interval has a cash cost

Consider an illustrative launch, unrelated to Karat’s actual terms. A supplier prepares 1,000 cases because the buyer expects a network rollout. Only one receiving location is ready and accepts 200. The remaining 800 cases still require storage and funding. The commercial opportunity has grown, but the cash tied to it has grown faster than the first delivery.

Who authorized that preparation matters. A firm purchase order, a forecast and a salesperson’s expectation should not trigger identical stock decisions. Before committing, agree which quantity the buyer will take, how changes are handled and when payment becomes due. A price negotiation that omits those questions leaves part of the launch’s economics unresolved.

A repeat order should explain itself

The first delivery tests the agreed setup. A repeat can show that consumption is developing, or simply that the buyer is filling another location. Those interpretations matter when planning capacity. The supplier should be able to distinguish routine replenishment, a rollout to new sites and a one-off stock build.

Look at the relationship at a product-and-location level. Does the second order arrive when expected? Are accepted quantities turning into invoices that clear? How much handling, urgent freight and support does each order require? Revenue alone cannot show whether the relationship is becoming easier to serve or merely more expensive to maintain.

What would change our assessment

The next useful evidence would connect the additional business to repeat shipments, accepted invoices and the effort required to fulfil them. A broad rollout with predictable replenishment would support a different reading from slow activation across many products. We do not have that evidence for these accounts.

Karat’s announcement is a starting point for this analysis. Its chronology shows why a supplier needs several measures of progress. Commercial approval tells you that a buyer sees a reason to work together. A functioning account also needs a launch schedule, funded stock, a route through receiving and payment, and a reason for the next order.

Scope of this analysis

The account chronology is company-reported. Subsequent sections are B(ornot)2B’s analysis. The 1,000-case example is hypothetical. No chain-specific procedures, contract terms or customer outcomes are inferred.
